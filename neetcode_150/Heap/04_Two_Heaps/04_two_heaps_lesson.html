Two Heaps

Keep a growing set of numbers split at its middle. A max-heap holds the smaller half and shows its largest value; a min-heap holds the larger half and shows its smallest. The two tops sit on either side of the middle, so the median is always one or two reads away, and each new number costs only a few heap operations.

Core idea: max-heap low | min-heap high Method 1: two heaps, O(log n) add, O(1) median Method 2: one sorted list, O(n) add Interactive traces synced to code

Problem in this pattern: Find Median from Data Stream · pattern notes (README) · look-alikes: Top K, Partition Search

Canonical example

Find Median from Data Stream: add 5, 15, 1, 3, 8 one at a time and ask for the median after each add.

Medians after each add: 5.0, 10.0, 5.0, 4.0, 5.0

Why this pattern matters

Live median latency

A service records response times as requests finish. A dashboard shows the median after every request without re-sorting the history each time.

Smoothing a noisy signal

A median over the last k sensor readings ignores single spikes. That is Sliding Window Median: the same two halves, plus removing the reading that leaves the window.

Two pools that trade items

Some greedy problems keep "not yet usable" items in one heap and "usable" items in another, moving items across as a threshold changes (IPO is the NeetCode-adjacent example).

Instructor note: the whole pattern is three heap moves per insert. What makes it hard in interviews is not the code but keeping two invariants straight: every value in low is at most every value in high, and low has the same size as high or one more.

What is the two heaps pattern?

Simple definition

Store the numbers in two heaps that meet at the middle. low is a max-heap of the smaller half, so its top is the largest small value. high is a min-heap of the larger half, so its top is the smallest large value. A heap gives its top in O(1) and takes a push or pop in O(log n), so keeping the split costs O(log n) per insert.

The tell in a problem statement

A median or middle value asked for repeatedly while numbers keep arriving: "data stream", "running median", "after each insertion". More broadly: a boundary between a lower and an upper group that moves as items come in.

Beginner shortcut: you never need the whole stream sorted, only the two values touching the middle. Two heaps keep exactly those two values on top. Python's heapq is a min-heap only, so low stores -x and you read its top as -low[0].

Step-by-step thinking process

1

Name the halves

low = max-heap (stored negated), high = min-heap. Decide that low may hold the one extra value.

2

Route through low

Push the new number into low, then move low's top into high. Now nothing in low is bigger than anything in high.

3

Fix the sizes

If len(high) > len(low), move high's top back. Sizes are now equal or low has one more.

4

Read the tops

Odd count: -low[0]. Even count: (-low[0] + high[0]) / 2. No loop, no sort.

How the stream becomes two heaps

The input is a sequence of addNum calls. Each call changes the two heaps by at most three moves; nothing else is stored. Below, each heap is drawn in sorted order so the split is easy to see; Python actually stores them as heap-ordered lists (shown in the trace's state boxes).

Input stream

addNum(5); addNum(15); addNum(1) addNum(3); addNum(8) findMedian() after every add
→

Two heaps after the last add

low = [-5, -1, -3] # values 1, 3, 5 top 5 high = [8, 15] # values 8, 15 top 8 sizes 3 / 2 -> odd -> median = 5.0

add 5: [5] | [], median 5.0

5 enters low, crosses to high, and comes back because high got bigger. The first number always ends in low.

add 15: [5] | [15], median 10.0

15 enters low, is its largest, crosses to high. Sizes 1 / 1, no rebalance. Even count: (5 + 15) / 2.

add 1: [1, 5] | [15], median 5.0

1 enters low, but low's largest is 5, so 5 crosses. high now has 2 values, so 5 comes straight back.

add 3: [1, 3] | [5, 15], median 4.0

3 enters low; 5 crosses to high. Sizes 2 / 2: the old median 5 is now high's top.

add 8: [1, 3, 5] | [8, 15], median 5.0

8 enters low, crosses to high, and high's smallest (5) comes back to low.

Critical point: routing every number through low first means you never write a comparison yourself. The heap decides which value is largest, and that value is the one that crosses. A number can land on the right side even if it was "added to low".

Method 1: two heaps (Find Median from Data Stream)

The template, line for line from the notebook's MedianFinder. Each addNum is three steps in the trace (push, cross, size check), and each findMedian is one more.

import heapq class MedianFinder: """Keep the numbers split into a low half (max-heap) and a high half (min-heap).""" def __init__(self): self.low = [] # max-heap of the smaller half (Python heapq is min, so store negatives) self.high = [] # min-heap of the larger half def addNum(self, num: int) -> None: heapq.heappush(self.low, -num) # tentatively add to the low half heapq.heappush(self.high, -heapq.heappop(self.low)) # move its largest into the high half if len(self.high) > len(self.low): # keep the halves balanced in size heapq.heappush(self.low, -heapq.heappop(self.high)) def findMedian(self) -> float: if len(self.low) > len(self.high): return float(-self.low[0]) # odd count -> top of the bigger half return (-self.low[0] + self.high[0]) / 2 # even count -> average of both tops

Key mental model

A bar splits the numbers in two. The left pile shows its biggest value at the bar, the right pile its smallest. Every insert goes in on the left and the left pile hands its biggest across the bar; if the right pile is then bigger, it hands its smallest back.

Common mistake

Forgetting the negation on one of the moves. low stores -x, so line 10's -heapq.heappop(self.low) turns it back into x before it enters high, and line 12 negates again on the way back. Miss one sign and the order invariant silently breaks.

Why heaps

The median only ever needs the largest of the small half and the smallest of the large half. A heap gives exactly its top in O(1) and keeps it correct after a push or pop in O(log n), without keeping the rest in order.

Method 2: one sorted list (the approach it replaces)

The notebook's MedianFinderInsort: keep a single list sorted at all times with bisect.insort. The median becomes an index lookup, but each insert shifts every value to the right of its slot. Traced on the same stream.

class MedianFinderInsort: """Better: the list is always sorted, so the median is an index lookup.""" def __init__(self): self.nums = [] def addNum(self, num: int) -> None: bisect.insort(self.nums, num) # O(log n) to find the slot, O(n) to shift the tail right def findMedian(self) -> float: n = len(self.nums) mid = n // 2 if n % 2: return float(self.nums[mid]) # O(1): direct index into the sorted list return (self.nums[mid - 1] + self.nums[mid]) / 2

Key mental model

A bookshelf kept in order. Finding where a new book goes is quick (look in the middle, then halve), but every book to its right has to slide over by one.

Common mistake

Calling it O(log n) per insert because bisect is binary search. The search is O(log n); list.insert is O(n). On a decreasing stream every value lands at index 0 and the whole list shifts.

Why it is not enough

In the notebook's benchmark (decreasing stream, a median after every add) the sorted list's time grew roughly 3 to 4.5 times per doubling of n, while the two heaps grew about 2 times. The shift is a fast memory move, so it only dominates once n reaches the thousands.

Edge cases you should test

Case A: a single number

addNum(42), then findMedian()

Median = 42.0

42 goes into low, crosses to high, and comes back because high is bigger. Sizes 1 / 0, so the odd branch reads -low[0]. high is empty, which is why the odd case must never touch high[0].

Case B: the LeetCode example

addNum(1), addNum(2), findMedian(), addNum(3), findMedian()

Outputs: 1.5, then 2.0

After 1, 2 the heaps are [1] | [2]: even, (1 + 2) / 2 = 1.5. After 3 they are [1, 2] | [3]: odd, low's top is 2. The notebook replays this exact operation list for all three approaches.

Case C: negatives and duplicates

addNum(-7) three times, then addNum(10)

Median = -7.0 after every add

Final heaps [-7, -7] | [-7, 10]. Equal values may sit on both sides of the bar; the invariant is ≤, not <. low stores 7 for -7, so a wrong sign shows up immediately on this input.

Case D: a decreasing stream

5, 4, 3, 2, 1

Medians: 5.0, 4.5, 4.0, 3.5, 3.0

Final heaps [1, 2, 3] | [4, 5]. Each new value is the smallest so far. The heaps still do at most three O(log n) moves, while the sorted list inserts at index 0 every time, which is why the notebook's benchmark uses this shape.

How to derive this in an interview

Reasoning path

Sorting on every query works but is at least O(n) per median. A sorted list makes the median O(1) but the insert O(n).

Ask what the median actually reads: only the largest value of the lower half and the smallest of the upper half.

"Largest of a changing set" is a max-heap; "smallest of a changing set" is a min-heap. Keep one of each.

State the invariants (order across the bar, sizes equal or low one bigger), then write the three moves that restore them after each insert.

What to say out loud

  • "I only need the two values next to the middle, not the whole order."
  • "Lower half in a max-heap, upper half in a min-heap; in Python the max-heap stores negatives."
  • "Every number goes into low, low's top moves to high, and if high is bigger I move its top back."
  • "That is O(log n) per add, O(1) per median, O(n) space."

Common beginner coding mistakes

Two heaps

  • Dropping a minus sign on one of the moves between low (negated) and high.
  • Pushing straight into one heap without the cross step, so a large value can stay in low.
  • Letting high hold the extra value but reading low's top for an odd count.
  • Averaging with //: (3 + 4) // 2 is 3, not 3.5.

Sorted list and sort-on-query

  • Calling insort O(log n): the list shift is O(n).
  • Using sorted(nums) in findMedian and then reading the middle of the unsorted nums.
  • Picking nums[n // 2] for an even count instead of averaging nums[n // 2 - 1] and nums[n // 2].
  • Benchmarking on random input only: a decreasing stream is the worst case for the shift.

Practice questions

This pattern has a single NeetCode problem, so questions 2 to 5 are follow-up variants of it, not separate NeetCode problems. Try each before opening it. The interactive page is Find Median from Data Stream.

Practice 1 — Find Median from Data Stream: add 6, 10, 2, 6, 5. What are the heaps and the median after each add?

Hint: run the three moves by hand: into low, low's top to high, move back if high is bigger.

Answer: medians 6.0, 8.0, 6.0, 6.0, 6.0. Final heaps [2, 5, 6] | [6, 10]: the two 6s end up on both sides of the bar. Compare with the step-through on the problem page.

Practice 2 — Variant (LeetCode follow-up): every number is in 0..100. Can you beat O(log n) per add?

Hint: there are only 101 possible values. What if you just counted them?

Answer: keep count[0..100]. addNum is count[x] += 1, O(1). findMedian walks the counts until the running total passes the middle position(s): at most 101 buckets, so O(1) for a fixed range. (Not in the notebook; checked against statistics.median on random streams while writing this lesson.)

Practice 3 — Variant (LeetCode follow-up): 99% of the numbers are in 0..100. What changes?

Hint: the median of such a stream almost always lies inside 0..100.

Answer: keep the 101 buckets plus two counters (or small sorted lists) for values below 0 and above 100. Add the "below" count before walking the buckets. Only if the middle position falls outside the buckets do you need the outlier list itself.

Practice 4 — Variant: Sliding Window Median (LC 480). nums = [1, 3, -1, -3, 5, 3, 6, 7], k = 3. Why is the template not enough on its own?

Hint: each window step adds one value and removes another. Which heap operation is missing?

Answer: heapq cannot remove an arbitrary value cheaply. Keep the same two halves, but delete lazily: record the leaving value in a "to delete" counter, adjust a size balance, and pop it only when it reaches a top. Expected output: [1.0, -1.0, -1.0, 3.0, 5.0, 6.0] (checked with statistics.median on each window).

Practice 5 — Variant: IPO (LC 502). k = 2, w = 0, profits = [1, 2, 3], capital = [0, 1, 1]. How do two heaps show up?

Hint: one group of projects you cannot afford yet, one group you can. Which project do you start next?

Answer: projects sorted (or in a min-heap) by capital; each round, move every affordable project into a max-heap by profit, then take its top. Here: start the profit-1 project (w = 1), then the profit-3 project (w = 4). Answer 4. Same "two pools, move items across a boundary" shape, but the boundary is your capital, not a median.

Final takeaway

Keep the middle on top of two heaps. A max-heap for the low half and a min-heap for the high half. Every number goes into low, low's top crosses to high, and if high is bigger its top comes back. The sizes stay equal or low has one more, so the median is low's top or the average of both tops: O(log n) per add, O(1) per median.
